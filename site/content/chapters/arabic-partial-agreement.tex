%% title: Arabic partial agreement
%% category: phenomena

Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) verbs agree with their subjects in person and gender. When subjects precede verbs, they also agree in number, but when subjects follow verbs, this isn't true:

\pex \dref{msa-partial}
  \a \begingl \gla al-awlaad-u katab-ū fi-l-dafātir //
      \glb the-boy.\textsc{pl-nom} write.\textsc{pst}-3\textsc{m.pl} in-the-notebook.\textsc{pl} //
      \endgl
  \a \begingl \gla katab-a al-awlaad-u fi-l-dafātir //
      \glb write.\textsc{pst}-3\textsc{ms} the-boy.\textsc{pl-nom} in-the-notebook.\textsc{pl} //
      \glft `The boys wrote in the notebooks.' //
      \endgl
\xe

Most commonly, the difference between these constructions is taken to be the presence or absence of movement to specTP:

\pex \dref{msa-partial-trees}
  \a \begin{forest}
[TP
  [DP \\ \textit{al-awlaad-u}, roof]
  [T\1
    [T \\ \textit{katab-ū}, name=t-a]
    [VoiceP
      [Voice, name=voice-a]
      [\vP
        [\lv, name=v-a]
        [\rt{KTB}, name=root-a]]]]]
\draw[->] (root-a) to[out=south, in=south] (v-a);
\draw[->] (v-a) to[out=south, in=south] (voice-a);
\draw[->] (voice-a) to[out=south, in=south] (t-a);
\end{forest}
  \a \begin{forest}
[TP
  [T \\ \textit{kataba}, name=t-b]
  [VoiceP
    [DP \\ \textit{al-awlaad-u}, roof]
    [Voice\1
      [Voice, name=voice-b]
      [\vP
        [\lv, name=v-b]
        [\rt{KTB}, name=root-b]]]]]
\draw[->] (root-b) to[out=south, in=south] (v-b);
\draw[->] (v-b) to[out=south, in=south] (voice-b);
\draw[->] (voice-b) to[out=south, in=south] (t-b);
\end{forest}
\xe

However one analyzes this phenomenon in a labelling theory of agreement, it is clear that at least one functional head in the verbal head complex \{T, Voice, \lv, \rt{}\} must end up with the person and gender features of the subject DP. Little-\lv and \rt{}\footnote{If the lexical root can even host features to begin with.} are too low\footnote{This prompts a question of when the forward pass happens relative to this; if after, then \lv/\rt{} couldn't ever get the DP's features from concord.} to receive its features, and T can receive its features only if Voice has received them; therefore, Voice must receive the subject DP's person and gender features.

Similarly, whichever head in \{T, Voice, \lv, \rt{}\} realizes plural agreement must somehow receive a singular feature in one case, and a plural feature in the other. Little-\lv and \rt{} can never receive the subject's plural feature, because they are too low. The only ways Voice could realize a singular feature in any case would be if it came with [+sg] inherently, or if it was not \textbf{value-seeking} (it had the rule $\tau(\text{+pl}, \text{–}) = \text{–}$), both of which seem questionable and furthermore should not be impacted by the subject's movement to specTP.

Therefore, T must realize the plural feature. It is easy to see how T would get the plural feature in \gref[a]{msa-partial-trees}: it is simply inherited from the subject's generation position to VoiceP, up to TP, and then shared via \textbf{MP-sharing} down to T.

It is less clear why T would ever lack the plural feature. There are two possibilities: either T comes pre-loaded with a [–pl] feature, or in the non-raising cases there is an expletive in specTP, and this expletive has a [–pl] feature (but no person or gender features). In the first case, T would regain a plural feature in \gref[a]{msa-partial-trees} when the subject DP remerges into specTP, shares its features with TP, and those features are shared with T through MP-sharing. In the second case, the expletive's features are shared with TP and then T, and \gref[a]{msa-partial-trees} needs no modifications. Both theories require \textbf{MP-sharing} to mimic \textbf{Spec-Head Agreement} (as opposed to something like mother-sharing, which doesn't allow specifier features to influence head features); they just use the mechanism in opposite constructions.
