%% title: Incorporating agreement as labelling into Minimalist syntax
%% category: misc

\citet{deal2025} proposes a new theory of agreement, which she calls Interaction-Satisfaction theory. In addition, she argues for the `Strong Merge-Agree Hypothesis', in which Merge and Agree are the only syntactic operations, and all non-local processes (including e.g. NPI licensing) use Agree.

Deal surveys a number of developments in the syntax of agreement since Chomsky's \citeyearpar{chomsky2001} Probe-Goal framework was first proposed. These developments, she argues, have progressively rendered the original motivations of `interpretability' and `valuedness' theoretically redundant.

The developments she's referring to are:

\pex \a Superiority Effects: verbs agree with certain elements, but only copy some of their features.
    \a Default Agreement: the derivation doesn't crash when verbs fail to agree with anything.
    \a Hyperraising and Multiple Agreement: DPs which have already been agreed with can still agree again.
    \a Negative Concord, Parasitic Participles, and Sequence of Tense: agreement that seems upwards can just be downwards, if you forget about interpretability.
\xe

\Last[a] reduces the power of valuedness, \Last[b] and \Last[c] do away with Chomsky's Activity Condition and unteeth interpretability, and \Last[d] gets rid of any remaining motivation for interpretability.

There are two reasons why this paper is important to my thesis:

\pex \a If the strong Merge-Agree hypothesis is true, then to truly characterize Agree, one needs to bound \textit{all} long-distance phenomena in syntax.
    \a Perhaps even more crucially, Deal's theory turns Agree from an operation that is non-deterministic and may sometimes lead to derivation crash (i.e. if uninterpretable features are left at the end of a derivation), into one which is deterministic, and in which all operations are obligatory (as surmised in \citealt{preminger2014}).
\xe

\Last[b] is particularly important because it is the same move I am taking by trying to make TSL syntax derivational. I am attempting to turn a non-deterministic, crashable theory into an obligatory, deterministic one.
