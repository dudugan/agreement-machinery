Agree and the Probe

chapter 02 of 09 · 4 examples · 1 tree · 6 notes
Synopsis This chapter sets out the basic mechanism assumed throughout: an unvalued head searches downward for the closest matching goal and copies its values. We look at what counts as closest, what happens when the closest thing is the wrong kind of thing, and how fine-grained a probe's search can be.

2.1The basic configuration

Agreement is the clearest case in natural language of a dependency that is both obligatory and asymmetric1: one element carries features it does not interpret, and must obtain their values from another element that does. Following Chomsky (2000, 2001), this asymmetry is encoded directly. A head bearing unvalued φ-features (the probe) searches its c-command domain for the closest bearer of valued φ-features (the goal) and copies their values.

(1)
a.The keys to the cabinet are on the table.
b.*The keys to the cabinet is on the table.
c.%The key to the cabinets are on the table.

The contrast in (1a–b) is the familiar one. (1c) is more interesting: speakers produce it and often accept it, an attraction effect documented since Bock & Miller (1991). We return to it in Chapter 8. For now it is a reminder that the closest goal in linear terms is not always the closest in structural terms2.

In (2), T is merged with an unvalued set of φ-features, written uφ: __. Its search domain is its sister, vP. The first DP it encounters is the external argument in Spec,vP, which bears [3, pl]. These values are copied onto T, which is then spelled out as are. Only after valuation does the subject raise to Spec,TP. That ordering matters later, when we ask whether movement can feed agreement.

2.2Locality and intervention

Because search proceeds top-down, a goal closer to the probe blocks agreement with one further away. This defective intervention is most visible in Icelandic experiencer constructions, where a dative sits between T and a nominative3:

(3)
ÞaðEXPL virðist / *virðastseem.SG / seem.PL einhverjum mannisome man.DAT [hestarnirthe.horses.NOM verabe seinir]slow
‘It seems to some man that the horses are slow.’ (Holmberg & Hróarsdóttir 2003)

The dative is close enough to stop the probe but, being inherently case-marked, cannot value it, and T surfaces with default singular morphology. Preminger (2014) argues that this is not a crash but a legitimate outcome: the probe fails, and the derivation goes on4.

2.3What the probe is looking for

If probes are relativized to particular features, intervention should be selective. A probe searching only for [participant] should skip third-person DPs entirely and agree with a lower first- or second-person goal. This is the core insight of Béjar & Rezac (2009). We develop the relativized view in Chapter 3 and test it against long-distance agreement in §4.2, where the goal sits inside an infinitival complement, as in (1) of Chapter 4.

Throughout, I write φ5 for the bundle that T and v probe for. The examples so far make the probe look like a single, indivisible searcher. The rest of the book argues against that picture.

Phenomena · Chapter 4

Long-Distance Agreement

chapter 04 of 09 · 2 examples · 1 tree · 3 notes
Synopsis In Hindi-Urdu, a matrix verb can agree with the object of an embedded infinitive. We argue this is ordinary downward Agree (§2.1) operating across an unusually small complement.

4.1The puzzle

In (1a), the matrix verb chaah ‘want’ is feminine. Its own subject is ergative and cannot be agreed with, so the feminine value must come from ṭehnii ‘branch’, the object of the embedded verb1.

(1)
a.
Shahrukh-neShahrukh-ERG [ṭehniibranch.F kaaṭ-nii]cut-INF.F chaah-iiwant-PFV.F thiibe.PST.F.SG
b.
Shahrukh-neShahrukh-ERG [ṭehniibranch.F kaaṭ-naa]cut-INF.M chaah-aawant-PFV.M thaabe.PST.M.SG
‘Shahrukh had wanted to cut the branch.’ (Bhatt 2005)

Both variants are grammatical. What is excluded is mismatch: agreement on the infinitive and on the matrix verb covary2. Compare Tsez, where long-distance agreement is restricted to topics (Polinsky & Potsdam 2001).

4.2Restructuring and the size of the complement

The covariation follows if the infinitive is a bare vP with no phase boundary of its own, so the matrix probe can see straight into it. Structure (2) makes this explicit: there is no CP or TP layer between matrix T and the embedded object. This is the same downward search as in (2) of Chapter 2, only over a longer distance.

The prediction is that anything that adds a phase boundary, such as an overt complementizer or a finite embedded clause, should block long-distance agreement. Section 4.3 shows that it does.
